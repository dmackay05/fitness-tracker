Training Phase Guide▸
Your 13-week wave, and what each phase is actually for. Toggle it on/off under Dashboard → Phase Cycle.
1 · Base — Weeks 1–6
RPE 8–9 · moderate-to-high volume · 1–2 reps left in the tank
What it's for: Accumulating quality training volume near your MEV/MAV. This is where most of your actual muscle-building work happens — consistent sets, consistent progression, nothing maxed out.
Tips: Don't chase failure yet — leave 1–2 reps every set. Focus on hitting the top of your rep range with clean form before adding load. This phase is the foundation everything else in the wave builds on, so consistency matters more than intensity here.
2 · Max Effort — Weeks 7–10
RPE 9–10 · low volume (1–2 sets) · near/true failure · 3 min rest
What it's for: After 6 weeks of volume, this phase tests what that volume actually built — fewer sets, but each one pushed close to real failure. It's a strength/neural-drive stimulus, not a pump phase.
Tips: Rest longer than feels necessary (3 min) — these sets are meant to be near-maximal, and short rest just makes them fail from fatigue instead of true strength limits. Fewer total sets is expected and correct; don't add extra volume back in.
3 · Supercompensation — Weeks 11–12
RPE 8–9 · high reps (15–20) · high volume · 1.5 min rest
What it's for: Capitalizing on the strength built in Max Effort week with a high-rep, high-volume push — more metabolic stress, more pump, a different growth stimulus than either of the first two phases.
Tips: Lean into the burn — these sets are supposed to feel hard by rep 12–15 even at lighter loads. Mind-muscle connection matters more here than raw load. Short rest is intentional; don't stretch it out like the Max Effort phase.
4 · Deload — Week 13
RPE 6–7 · roughly half the usual volume
What it's for: Planned recovery before the wave repeats. Fatigue accumulates across the first 12 weeks even when you feel fine session to session — this week lets joints and connective tissue catch up before ramping intensity again.
Tips: Don't skip it just because you feel strong — that's usually exactly when it's most needed. Given your elbow and SI history, this week matters more for you than it would for someone with no joint flags. Keep loads light and let effort stay well short of failure.
Rehab and joint-protective work (elbow eccentric rehab, wrist curls, and other flagged movements) stays fixed at its normal prescription in every phase — it's not scaled up or down, since it's protective work rather than a hypertrophy stimulus.
Muscle Growth — What the Science Says▸
Only findings backed by multiple studies or meta-analyses. Each card is rated:
● Strong — several meta-analyses agree  ·  ● Promising — consistent studies, details still being refined  ·  ● Not supported — popular, but the evidence says no
1 · Effort matters more than load — Strong
Light and heavy loads build roughly the same muscle, anywhere from about 6 to 30+ reps, as long as sets end close to failure. Mechanical tension on the fibers is the driver, and a lighter weight gets there by recruiting more fibers as the set gets hard. Resistance bands produce strength gains comparable to free weights and machines.
In your plan: Bands are a legitimate tool, not a compromise. If a band feels "too light," push the set closer to failure before assuming you need a heavier one.
Evidence
Schoenfeld et al. 2017, J Strength Cond Res (meta-analysis, low vs. high load) · Lopez et al. 2021, Med Sci Sports Exerc (meta-analysis) · Lopes et al. 2019, SAGE Open Med (meta-analysis, elastic bands vs. conventional)
2 · Get close to failure, but you don't have to reach it — Strong
Growth increases as sets get closer to failure, so sets stopped with many reps left do little. Taking every set all the way to failure adds little extra growth, though, and costs a lot more fatigue. Ending most sets with about 0–3 reps left gets nearly all the benefit.
In your plan: This is why most sets target 1–3 RIR. People routinely underestimate how many reps they have left, which is why the plan includes a weekly anchor set taken to true failure.
Evidence
Refalo et al. 2023, Sports Med (meta-analysis, failure vs. non-failure) · Robinson et al. 2024, Sports Med (meta-regression, reps in reserve vs. hypertrophy) · Grgic et al. 2022, J Sport Health Sci (meta-analysis)
3 · Weekly volume has a dose-response — Strong
More hard sets per muscle per week produce more growth, with diminishing returns. Around 10+ weekly sets per muscle reliably beats fewer than 5. Each additional set adds a little less than the one before, and the right amount varies by person and by how well you're recovering.
In your plan: The MEV/MAV/MRV landmarks put this into practice. Add sets when progress stalls and recovery is good, and hold or cut them back when joints or sleep suffer.
Evidence
Schoenfeld, Ogborn & Krieger 2017, J Sports Sci (meta-analysis, weekly sets) · Krieger 2010, J Strength Cond Res (meta-analysis, single vs. multiple sets) · Pelland et al. 2024 (meta-regression, diminishing returns; preprint)
4 · Progressive overload is the long-term engine — Strong
Muscle adapts to what you ask of it, so the stimulus has to keep rising over months: more reps, more load, or more sets at the same effort. Beginners and people returning after a break progress fastest, and gains slow as training age increases.
In your plan: The log is what makes overload possible. The rule is to move up once you hit the top of the rep range at your target RIR for 2 sessions in a row. With bands, keep your stance distance consistent (tape-mark it), or the numbers stop meaning anything.
Evidence
ACSM Position Stand, Med Sci Sports Exerc 2009 · Plotkin et al. 2022, PeerJ (load vs. repetition progression — both work)
5 · Frequency is mostly a way to spread volume — Strong
When total weekly sets are equal, training a muscle once, twice, or three times a week produces similar growth. Splitting sets across more days mainly keeps each session shorter and the quality of each set higher.
In your plan: Hitting each muscle about twice a week (Upper A/B/C, Lower Push/Pull) is a practical way to fit the volume in, not a magic number.
Evidence
Schoenfeld, Grgic & Krieger 2019, J Sports Sci (meta-analysis, frequency) · Pelland et al. 2024 (meta-regression; frequency effect negligible once volume is accounted for; preprint)
6 · Protein: total daily intake, ~1.6 g/kg — Strong
Extra protein boosts muscle gain from training, and the benefit levels off around 1.6 g/kg/day. Going up to about 2.2 g/kg covers people who respond more strongly and helps preserve muscle in a deficit. Total daily intake matters far more than timing; the "30-minute anabolic window" is not supported.
In your plan: This is why the protein target scales with body weight. Spreading it across 3–4 meals is sensible, but hitting the daily number is what counts.
Evidence
Morton et al. 2018, Br J Sports Med (meta-analysis, 49 RCTs) · Schoenfeld, Aragon & Krieger 2013, J Int Soc Sports Nutr (meta-analysis, protein timing) · Jäger et al. 2017, ISSN Position Stand
7 · Energy balance sets the ceiling — Strong
Recomposition (losing fat and gaining muscle at the same time) is well documented, especially in people returning to training or carrying extra body fat. Larger deficits blunt muscle gain, with lean-mass gains noticeably reduced around a ~500 kcal/day deficit. Eating at maintenance or slightly above gives the most room to build.
In your plan: This backs the current choice to eat at maintenance or in a small surplus while prioritizing chest, arm, and leg growth.
Evidence
Murphy & Koehler 2022, Scand J Med Sci Sports (meta-analysis, energy deficit vs. lean mass) · Barakat et al. 2020, Strength Cond J (review, body recomposition)
8 · Cardio doesn't kill gains — Strong
Adding endurance work to resistance training doesn't meaningfully reduce muscle growth or maximal strength. The "interference effect" mostly shows up in explosive power, and when very high cardio volumes crowd out recovery.
In your plan: The walk-run intervals and Saturday rides are compatible with building muscle. Keeping hard cardio away from your hardest leg sessions is a sensible buffer.
Evidence
Schumann et al. 2022, Sports Med (meta-analysis, concurrent training)
9 · Sleep protects muscle — Strong
Sleep loss lowers muscle protein synthesis. In a calorie deficit, short sleep shifts more of the weight lost from fat to lean mass, even when diet is identical.
In your plan: Poor sleep is the most likely reason a week of lifts feels flat. Treat it as part of training, not something separate from it.
Evidence
Nedeltcheva et al. 2010, Ann Intern Med (RCT, sleep in a deficit) · Lamon et al. 2021, Physiol Rep (sleep deprivation and protein synthesis) · Saner et al. 2020, Mol Metab (sleep restriction and protein synthesis)
10 · Train muscles in the stretched position — Promising
Exercises that load a muscle at long lengths, such as overhead triceps extensions, Bayesian curls, deep flyes, and RDLs, tend to grow as much or more than versions that load the shortened position. Partial reps done in the stretched half often match full range of motion. The direction is consistent across studies; how large the advantage is, and whether it holds for every muscle, is still being worked out.
In your plan: This is the reasoning behind the stretch-focused variants and the door-anchor setups, where band tension stays high at the bottom instead of going slack.
Evidence
Maeo et al. 2023, Eur J Sport Sci (overhead vs. neutral triceps extension) · Pedrosa et al. 2022, Eur J Sport Sci (lengthened partials, quads) · Kassiano et al. 2023, J Strength Cond Res (calves) · Wolf et al. 2023, J Trial Error (meta-analysis, partial vs. full ROM)
11 · Rest long enough to do quality work — Promising
Very short rests (under ~60 s) tend to reduce growth, likely because the following sets suffer. Beyond about 90 s the extra benefit is small. Longer rests help most on big compound movements.
In your plan: Rest until you can hit your target reps again, usually 1.5–2 min on presses, rows, and hinges and about 1 min on isolation work.
Evidence
Schoenfeld et al. 2016, J Strength Cond Res (1 vs. 3 min rest) · Singer et al. 2024, Front Sports Act Living (meta-analysis, rest intervals)
12 · People respond very differently — Promising
On identical programs, some people gain several times more muscle than others. Genetics, sleep, age, and training history all contribute. Muscle regained after a break also comes back faster than it was first built.
In your plan: Compare yourself to your own log, not to anyone else. After a sporadic stretch, expect strength to return faster than it took to build.
Evidence
Hubal et al. 2005, Med Sci Sports Exerc (585 subjects, variability) · Staron et al. 1991, J Appl Physiol and Psilander et al. 2019, J Appl Physiol (retraining)
Not supported by the evidence
Super-slow reps. Rep durations from about 0.5 to 8 seconds build similar muscle. Very slow reps (10 s or more) do worse, and tempo only matters because it changes how close you get to failure. (Schoenfeld et al. 2015, Sports Med)
"Muscle confusion." Constantly changing exercises doesn't add growth, because consistent overload on well-chosen movements drives progress. Some exercise variety does help develop different regions of a muscle. (Baz-Valle et al. 2019, PLoS One)
The anabolic window. You don't need protein within 30 minutes of a workout; daily total dominates. (Schoenfeld et al. 2013)
"Toning" with light weight and high reps. "Toned" means muscle plus lower body fat. Light, easy sets that stop far from failure don't build much of either. (see cards 1–2)
Spot reduction. Training a body part doesn't preferentially burn the fat over it. (Vispute et al. 2011, J Strength Cond Res; Ramírez-Campillo et al. 2013, J Strength Cond Res)
Most of this research is on young and middle-aged adults over 6–16 week studies. Treat these findings as reliable principles rather than exact numbers, and let your own log settle the details.
Supplements — What Actually Works▸
Same ratings as above. Supplements add a small amount on top of training, protein, and sleep; they don't replace any of them.
Creatine monohydrate — Strong
The most-studied sports supplement. Taken alongside resistance training, it modestly increases strength, lean mass, and muscle growth by letting you do a bit more work per set. 3–5 g/day, any time of day, every day. A loading phase isn't needed; it only gets you saturated sooner (about 1 week instead of 3–4). Plain monohydrate is what the research used, and pricier forms haven't been shown to beat it.
Safety: In healthy people, long-term studies show no kidney or liver harm. The "creatine causes hair loss" claim comes from a single 2009 study that measured a hormone, not hair, and it has not been replicated. Anyone with existing kidney disease should ask their doctor first.
For your tracking: Expect the scale to go up about 1–3 lb in the first few weeks from water stored inside the muscle. That isn't fat, so lean on the waist tape and photos during that stretch.
Evidence
Kreider et al. 2017, ISSN Position Stand, J Int Soc Sports Nutr · Antonio et al. 2021, J Int Soc Sports Nutr (common questions and misconceptions) · Lanhers et al. 2017, Sports Med (meta-analysis, strength) · Burke et al. 2023, Nutrients (meta-analysis, hypertrophy) · van der Merwe et al. 2009, Clin J Sport Med (the unreplicated DHT study)
Caffeine — Strong (performance)
Reliably improves strength, reps to failure, and endurance by a small amount. The studied dose is 3–6 mg per kg of body weight about 30–60 min before training, and many people get most of the effect from less. It improves performance in a session rather than building muscle directly.
Trade-off: Caffeine late in the day costs sleep (card 9 above), which usually outweighs the performance boost. Keep it earlier in the day.
Word of Wisdom: The Church specifically names coffee and tea and does not address other caffeine sources, so what you use here is your own decision. Herbal teas are fine.
Evidence
Guest et al. 2021, ISSN Position Stand, J Int Soc Sports Nutr · Grgic et al. 2019, Br J Sports Med (umbrella review) · Grgic et al. 2018, J Int Soc Sports Nutr (meta-analysis, strength and power)
Protein powder — Strong (as food)
Whey, casein, and good plant blends work exactly as well as protein from food, because it's the same protein in a more convenient form. It counts toward the daily total from card 6 and does nothing beyond that.
Evidence
Morton et al. 2018, Br J Sports Med · Jäger et al. 2017, ISSN Position Stand
Worth knowing, but narrow — Promising
Beta-alanine. Helps with hard efforts lasting about 1–4 minutes by buffering the "burn." Its effect on typical hypertrophy sets is small, and it causes a harmless tingling. (Saunders et al. 2017, Br J Sports Med, meta-analysis)
Dietary nitrate (beets, beetroot juice). Gives a small boost to endurance performance, less consistently in trained athletes. It's more relevant to the rides than to lifting. (Senefeld et al. 2020, Med Sci Sports Exerc, meta-analysis)
Vitamin D. Helps muscle and strength mainly if you're deficient, which is common in northern-latitude winters. A blood test is the way to find out rather than guessing. (Zhang et al. 2019, Nutrients, meta-analysis)
Not supported by the evidence
BCAAs. They add nothing when daily protein is adequate, since whole protein already contains them along with the other essential amino acids BCAAs lack. (Wolfe 2017, J Int Soc Sports Nutr; Plotkin et al. 2021, Nutrients)
"Testosterone boosters" (tribulus, fenugreek blends, and similar). Most show no meaningful effect on testosterone or muscle in men with normal levels. (Clemesha et al. 2020, World J Mens Health; Qureshi et al. 2014, J Diet Suppl)
Glutamine for muscle. No added strength or muscle in healthy lifters. (Candow et al. 2001, Eur J Appl Physiol)
HMB in trained people. Early positive findings haven't held up in well-controlled trials. (Jakubowski et al. 2020, Nutrients, meta-analysis)
Collagen as a muscle protein. It's low in leucine and missing tryptophan, so it builds less muscle than whey gram for gram. Don't count it at full value toward your protein target. (Oikawa et al. 2018, Br J Nutr)
Supplements aren't tested by the FDA before they're sold, and some have been found contaminated or mislabeled. Look for third-party certification (NSF Certified for Sport or Informed Sport). Check with a doctor before starting anything if you take prescription medications.

📈Training CyclePhases, deload, split, rotation›
📈 Training Phase Cycle
13-week wave adapted from Jeff Nippard's Ultimate PPL: Phase 1 Base (wk 1-6, moderate volume, RPE 8-9/9-10) → Phase 2 Max Effort (wk 7-10, 1-2 sets, RPE 9-10) → Phase 3 Supercompensation (wk 11-12, high volume, 15-20 reps) → Deload (wk 13), then repeats. Every exercise's sets/reps/RPE/rest updates automatically as the weeks pass. Rehab and joint-flagged movements (elbow work, wrist curls, SI-flagged moves) are excluded on purpose — they stay fixed no matter the phase.
Manual phase override (optional)
⏸️ Deload Week
For a high-volume or joint-stress week. Drops your step goal ~35% for 7 days as a visual reminder — you're still responsible for cutting lift load ~30-40%, skipping deep stretch-position work, keeping rides easy/flat, and leaning into yoga. Auto-turns off after 7 days.
🏋️ Full-Body Mode
Swaps the Upper/Lower split for full-body sessions Mon · Wed · Fri, with short ~20-min yoga flows + walk-run recovery Tue · Thu, ride Saturday, rest Sunday. Same exercises and ids, so logs and progression carry over. Each exercise is capped at 2–3 sets per session since every muscle gets hit three times a week. Toggle back any time.
🔁 Session Rotation
Shifts the four resistance sessions one weekday per week, so Lower Strength isn't always Friday. Wednesday recovery, Saturday ride and Sunday rest stay put.
🔄 Variant Rotation
Auto-cycles each exercise's variants (including the base move) one slot per week, so options you never manually swap to still get used. On by default — this overrides any manual swap, so turn it off if you need to lock an exercise to a specific variant (e.g. avoiding one for your elbow or SI joint).

▶️ Maintenance Mode
A maintenance block — sits at your measured maintenance instead of the deficit, for as long as you leave it on. Counters metabolic adaptation from sustained dieting and lets training intensity/recovery catch up. Tracks your measured maintenance as it updates, and stays on until you manually turn it off. Keep protein at 170g+ and train as normal.
⬆️ Bulk Phase
A deliberate muscle-building surplus — sits at your measured maintenance plus a modest surplus (default +350 kcal) for as long as you leave it on, and bumps protein to 190g. Keep the surplus lean-leaning (fiber-dense carbs, unsaturated fats) over saturated fat/added sugar. Mutually exclusive with Maintenance Mode. Stays on until you turn it off — check in with your doctor/dietitian on A1c and cholesterol during this phase.
